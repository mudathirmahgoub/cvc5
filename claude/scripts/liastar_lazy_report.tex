\begin{abstract}
This document explains the algorithms behind the lazy decision procedure
for the \emph{additive closure} (star) operator in \cvc, and the sequence
of improvements that took a benchmark family from 450/480 solved to
480/480 (median 45\,ms per instance). It is written to be self-contained:
every concept is defined in plain language when it first appears, every
definition and every claim comes with a small worked example placed right
where it is used, and one \emph{running example} is carried through the
whole document. Negative experimental results are included, because two of
them supplied the machinery for the final breakthroughs.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}

\subsection{Vectors, sums, and the star of a set}

We work with vectors of non-negative integers, written
$\vec{x} = (x_1,\ldots,x_n)$. Vectors are added coordinate-wise:
$(1,0) + (0,2) = (1,2)$.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Running example} (used throughout this document). Let $p$ be the
predicate
\[
p(x,y) \;=\; \big(\underbrace{x \ge 1 \wedge y = 0}_{\text{``horizontal''}}\big)
\;\vee\;
\big(\underbrace{x = 0 \wedge y \ge 2}_{\text{``vertical''}}\big),
\]
and let $\Sset$ be the set of non-negative integer vectors satisfying it:
\[
\Sset \;=\; \{(1,0),\,(2,0),\,(3,0),\,\ldots\} \;\cup\; \{(0,2),\,(0,3),\,(0,4),\ldots\}.
\]
\end{exbox}

The \emph{star} of $\Sset$, written $\Sstar$, is the set of all vectors
obtainable by adding finitely many members of $\Sset$ (taking \emph{zero}
members is allowed, which produces the zero vector $\vzero$):
\[
\Sstar \;=\; \Big\{\, \vec{s}_1 + \cdots + \vec{s}_k \;\Big|\; k \ge 0,\ \vec{s}_j \in \Sset \,\Big\}.
\]

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} In the running example:
\begin{itemize}
\item $(3,7) \in \Sstar$, because $(3,7) = (3,0) + (0,7)$, and both
  summands satisfy $p$.
\item $(0,0) \in \Sstar$: it is the empty sum. Note $(0,0) \notin \Sset$
  itself --- the star always contains $\vzero$ even when the set does not.
\item $(0,1) \notin \Sstar$: every member of $\Sset$ with a nonzero second
  coordinate has $y \ge 2$, and coordinates never decrease when adding
  non-negative vectors, so no sum can reach $y = 1$.
\end{itemize}
So $\Sstar = \{(0,0)\} \cup \{(x,0) \mid x \ge 1\} \cup \{(0,y) \mid y \ge 2\}
\cup \{(x,y) \mid x \ge 1,\, y \ge 2\}$.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{The \texttt{int.star-contains} literal, and where it comes from}

\cvc accepts the operator
\[
\ell \;=\; \mathit{int.star\mbox{-}contains}\big(\lambda x\, y.\; p(x,y),\;\; v_1,\, v_2\big),
\]
a formula that is true exactly when the vector $\vv = (v_1,v_2)$, built
from the integer terms $v_1, v_2$ of the input problem, belongs to
$\Sstar$. The first argument is a \emph{lambda term}: an anonymous function
binding the variables $x,y$ of the predicate, so that the predicate can be
passed around as a value.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (where such constraints come from).} Consider sets $A, B$
over some universe and the constraint $|A| = 3 \wedge |A \cap B| = 0$.
Every element of the universe falls into exactly one of four \emph{regions}
(in $A$ only, in $B$ only, in both, in neither). Summing, per region, the
0/1 indicator vector of each element shows that the vector of region
cardinalities is a \emph{sum of per-element vectors}, each satisfying a
fixed predicate --- i.e.\ a member of a star set. This is how Boolean
algebra with Presburger arithmetic (BAPA, sets with cardinalities) and its
multiset variant (MAPA) are encoded; our benchmarks come from such
encodings, where the predicates contain many \textsf{ite} (if-then-else)
terms defining indicator values.
\end{exbox}

Deciding $\ell$ means answering: \emph{can $\vv$ be written as a sum of
vectors that each satisfy $p$?} The rest of this document is about doing
that inside an SMT solver.

\subsection{SMT vocabulary used in this document}

For readers from neighbouring areas we fix the few solver terms we need.
An SMT solver couples a SAT solver (handling the Boolean structure) with
\emph{theory solvers} (handling, e.g., linear integer arithmetic, LIA). A
\emph{lemma} is a formula a theory solver hands to the SAT solver to be
conjoined with the problem; it must not change satisfiability.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (lemma).} The arithmetic solver may emit
$x \ge 1 \Rightarrow x \ge 0$ --- valid, so always safe. A lemma may also
introduce \emph{fresh} symbols: $d \Rightarrow x = 2$ with a brand-new
Boolean $d$ is safe even though it is not valid, because any model can be
extended by choosing $d = \false$. Such lemmas are called
\emph{satisfiability-preserving}.
\end{exbox}

A \emph{candidate model} is the assignment the solver currently believes
satisfies everything; theory solvers inspect it at \emph{full effort} (when
the Boolean search has assigned all variables) and either accept it or emit
new lemmas. The SAT solver maintains a \emph{trail} of variable
assignments; each is either a \emph{decision} (a guess) or a
\emph{propagation} (forced by a clause). The number of decisions on the
trail is the \emph{decision level}; anything implied with no decisions at
all is \emph{fixed at level~0} --- it holds in every model.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (trail, propagation, decision level).} Clauses:
$(a \vee b)$, $(\neg a \vee c)$. The solver \emph{decides} $a = \true$
(level~1); the second clause then \emph{propagates} $c = \true$ at
level~1. Nothing here is fixed at level~0: a different decision
($a=\false$) is still possible. If instead the input contained the unit
clause $(a)$, then $a$ would be fixed at level~0.
\end{exbox}

Finally, a \emph{subsolver} is a separate, private SMT solver instance that
a theory creates for its own queries; \emph{incremental} means assertions
can be added between satisfiability checks; and checking with
\emph{assumptions} means temporarily assuming extra literals for a single
check, without asserting them permanently.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (assumptions and unsat core).} Assert $x \ge 1$
permanently; then ask \texttt{checkSat} under assumptions
$\{x \le 0,\ y \ge 5\}$. The answer is \textsf{unsat}, and the solver can
report \emph{which assumptions were actually used}: the core
$\{x \le 0\}$. The assumption $y \ge 5$ played no role. This
``unsat-assumptions core'' feature is used twice in this document.
\end{exbox}

\section{The reduction to linear arithmetic}

\subsection{Cells: the predicate in disjunctive normal form}

A formula is in \emph{disjunctive normal form} (DNF) if it is a disjunction
(or-list) of conjunctions (and-lists) of atomic constraints. Each
conjunction of linear constraints describes a convex polyhedron; we call
the polyhedra of the predicate's DNF its \emph{cells}.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} The running example
$p(x,y) = (x \ge 1 \wedge y = 0) \vee (x = 0 \wedge y \ge 2)$
is already in DNF and has two cells: the horizontal ray and the vertical
ray. A predicate that is \emph{not} in DNF, such as
$(x \ge 1 \vee y \ge 1) \wedge (x \le 5)$, is converted by distributing:
$(x \ge 1 \wedge x \le 5) \vee (y \ge 1 \wedge x \le 5)$ --- two cells.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (why \textsf{ite} terms blow the DNF up).} The equation
$u = \mathsf{ite}(x \ge 1,\, 1,\, 0)$ (``$u$ is $1$ if $x \ge 1$, else
$0$'') becomes the disjunction
$(x \ge 1 \wedge u = 1) \vee (x \le 0 \wedge u = 0)$: one \textsf{ite}
doubles the number of cells. Two independent \textsf{ite}s give
$2 \times 2 = 4$ cells, and the hard benchmark predicates contain up to
\emph{23} \textsf{ite}s --- up to $2^{23} \approx 8.4$ million potential
cells. This is why the \emph{eager} strategy below fails and a lazy one is
needed.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{Describing one cell: generators and rays}

To turn a cell into arithmetic, we need a finite description of its
infinitely many integer points. The library \normaliz provides one: a
finite set of \emph{base points} $g$ (called module generators) and a
finite set of \emph{directions} $\vec{h}$ (the Hilbert basis of the cell's
\emph{recession cone} --- the set of directions in which one can walk
forever without leaving the cell). Every integer point of the cell is
$g + \sum_k l_k \vec{h}_k$ for some base point and non-negative integers
$l_k$.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} The horizontal cell $x \ge 1 \wedge y = 0$ has one base
point $g = (1,0)$ and one direction $\vec{h} = (1,0)$: its points are
$(1,0) + l \cdot (1,0) = (1{+}l,\, 0)$, i.e.\ $(1,0), (2,0), (3,0), \ldots$
The vertical cell has base point $(0,2)$ and direction $(0,1)$.

A two-direction example: the cell $x \ge y \wedge y \ge 0$ (a quarter-plane
tilted onto the diagonal) has base point $(0,0)$ and Hilbert basis
$\{(1,0),\, (1,1)\}$; e.g.\ $(3,2) = (0,0) + 1\cdot(1,0) + 2\cdot(1,1)$.
Note $(1,1)$ is needed: it cannot be built from $(1,0)$ and any other
single direction inside the cell.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{The star constraints}

How do we express ``$\vv$ is a sum of \emph{several} points of a cell''?
Take the cell's base point $g$ with a multiplier $\mu \ge 0$ meaning
\emph{how many summands} are drawn from this cell, and ray multipliers
$l_k \ge 0$ accumulating all the ray steps of all those summands; the cell
contributes $\mu \cdot g + \sum_k l_k \vec{h}_k$. The side condition $\mu =
0 \Rightarrow l_k = 0$ says rays are only available if at least one summand
was actually taken. Summing the contributions of all cells and equating
with $\vv$ gives the \emph{star constraints}.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} For the running example
($p(x,y) = (x \ge 1 \wedge y = 0) \vee (x = 0 \wedge y \ge 2)$) the star
constraints over the fresh integer unknowns $\mu_1,l_1,\mu_2,l_2$ are
\[
v_1 = \mu_1 \cdot 1 + l_1 \cdot 1, \qquad
v_2 = \mu_2 \cdot 2 + l_2 \cdot 1, \qquad
\mu_i, l_i \ge 0, \qquad
\mu_i = 0 \Rightarrow l_i = 0 .
\]
$\vv \in \Sstar$ \emph{iff} these constraints are satisfiable:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\vv = (3,7)$: take $\mu_1 = 1, l_1 = 2$ (one horizontal summand,
  stretched by 2) and $\mu_2 = 1, l_2 = 5$. Satisfiable --- and indeed
  $(3,7) = (3,0) + (0,7) \in \Sstar$.
\item $\vv = (0,1)$: $v_2 = 1$ forces $\mu_2 = 0$ (one vertical summand
  already contributes $2$), hence $l_2 = 0$, hence $v_2 = 0 \ne 1$.
  Unsatisfiable --- and indeed $(0,1) \notin \Sstar$.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

The procedure can therefore emit the single lemma
$\ell \Leftrightarrow \mathit{star}$ and let the ordinary LIA engine decide
everything. The \emph{eager} strategy does exactly this, building all
cells up front --- and dies on the $2^{23}$-cell predicates, as explained
above. Everything from here on is about avoiding the full DNF.

\section{The lazy loop}

The lazy strategy discovers cells \emph{one at a time}, maintaining an
\emph{under-approximation}: a version of the star constraints,
$\mathit{star}_k$, built from only the $k$ cells found so far. Since
missing cells can only \emph{remove} ways of summing,
$\mathit{star}_k$ describes a \emph{subset} of $\Sstar$.

The discovery engine is a subsolver holding
\[
\mathit{base}(\vy) \;=\; p(\vy) \wedge \vy \ge 0
\]
over fresh constants $\vy = (y_1,\ldots,y_n)$. (Fresh constants are needed
because a subsolver cannot be given a formula with the lambda's bound
variables; replacing bound variables by fresh constants is standard and
preserves satisfiability --- e.g.\ asking whether $\lambda x.\,x{\ge}1$ is
satisfiable becomes asking whether $y \ge 1$ is, for a new symbol $y$.)

Each \emph{round} of the loop does the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Run \texttt{checkSat} on the subsolver, which also holds the
  negations of all previously found cells. If \textsf{unsat}, every cell is
  covered: emit the \emph{exact} lemma $\ell \Leftrightarrow \mathit{star}$
  and stop. (This exactness is what makes \textsf{unsat} answers for $\ell$
  possible at all.)
\item Otherwise read the subsolver's model and extract the cell containing
  it, by fixing every atomic constraint of $\mathit{base}$ to the truth
  value it has in the model.
\item Hand the cell to \normaliz, extend the star constraints to
  $\mathit{star}_k$, and emit the \emph{guarded} lemma
  $g_k \Rightarrow (\ell \Leftrightarrow \mathit{star}_k)$, where $g_k$ is
  a fresh Boolean. Also emit $\neg g_{k-1}$, retiring the previous round's
  guard. The solver is told to \emph{prefer} $g_k = \true$ when it gets to
  choose (a \emph{phase preference}: a hint for decisions, never a
  constraint).
\item Assert the negation of the new cell in the subsolver, so the next
  round's model lies somewhere new.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (two rounds on the running example).} Round 1: the
subsolver holds $\mathit{base}$ only; say its model is $\vy = (4,0)$.
The atoms of $\mathit{base}$ get the values $x{\ge}1 \mapsto \true$,
$y{=}0 \mapsto \true$, $x{=}0 \mapsto \false$, $y{\ge}2 \mapsto \false$,
giving the cell $D_1 = (x \ge 1 \wedge y = 0 \wedge \neg(x{=}0) \wedge y < 2)$
--- the horizontal cell, plus two redundant conjuncts (cleaned up in the
next section). We emit $g_1 \Rightarrow (\ell \Leftrightarrow
\mathit{star}_1)$ where $\mathit{star}_1$ is
$v_1 = \mu_1 + l_1 \wedge v_2 = 0 \wedge \ldots$, and assert $\neg D_1$ in
the subsolver. Round 2: the subsolver's model must avoid $D_1$, say
$\vy = (0,2)$; we discover the vertical cell, emit $\neg g_1$ and
$g_2 \Rightarrow (\ell \Leftrightarrow \mathit{star}_2)$, and the
subsolver becomes \textsf{unsat} in round 3: enumeration complete.
\end{exbox}

\why{Why guard the lemma instead of asserting
$\ell \Leftrightarrow \mathit{star}_k$ outright? Because
$\mathit{star}_k$ is only an under-approximation. Concretely: after
round~1 above, $\mathit{star}_1$ forces $v_2 = 0$, so for an input where
$\vv = (0,5)$ the unguarded equivalence would force $\ell = \false$ ---
but $(0,5) \in \Sstar$ via the vertical cell, so on an input asserting
$\ell$ the solver would wrongly answer \textsf{unsat}. With the guard, the
SAT solver escapes by setting $g_1 = \false$ (the lemma
$g_1 \Rightarrow \cdots$ is then trivially satisfied), and the loop
refines further. In the other direction no guard is needed: if the
candidate model satisfies $p[\vv]$ ($\vv$ itself is a one-summand member)
or satisfies $\mathit{star}_k$ (a $k$-cell sum exists), then
$\vv \in \Sstar$ really holds --- an under-approximation can never wrongly
certify \textsf{sat}. The procedure accepts a model exactly in these two
cases (the \emph{model-value shortcut}).}

\section{Four refinement strategies, and what they taught us}

This section reports engineering variants of the loop. Two are negative
results; we keep them because their machinery returns later, and because
they redirected the investigation to the real bottlenecks.

\subsection{Push/pop refinement}

In the baseline, the subsolver accumulates one negated cell per round:
after three rounds it holds $\mathit{base}, \neg D_1, \neg D_2, \neg D_3$,
and these assertions live forever. The push/pop variant instead keeps a
single combined formula: since
$\neg(D_1 \vee D_2 \vee D_3)$ implies $\neg(D_1 \vee D_2)$, each round can
\emph{pop} (retract) the previous combined formula and assert the new one,
keeping exactly two live assertions --- the hope being lower memory in the
subsolver.

\emph{Measured result:} no gain. On the hardest instance, both variants
time out with the \emph{same} ${\sim}3.2$\,GB --- profiling showed the
memory lived in the \emph{main} solver's accumulated lemmas, not in the
subsolver. On the full suite, push/pop solves slightly \emph{fewer} (460
vs.\ 463 of 480): popping throws away everything the subsolver had
\emph{learned} about already-covered regions, and the cumulative formula
must be re-processed from scratch each round.
\textbf{Lesson: measure the bottleneck before optimizing it.}

\subsection{Enumerating inside the main solver}

A more radical variant deletes the subsolver and lets the \emph{main}
search enumerate cells: the fresh constants $\vy$ are added to the main
problem, constrained by lemmas
$h_0 \Rightarrow \mathit{base}(\vy)$ and, per discovered cell,
$h_k \Rightarrow h_{k-1}$ and $h_k \Rightarrow \neg D_k(\vy)$, where the
$h_k$ are fresh Booleans (so activating $h_k$ places $\vy$ in a
not-yet-covered cell). When the candidate model sets $h_k = \true$, the
values of $\vy$ are read off and a new cell is harvested.

\why{Three problems appeared, each with a concrete failure.

\emph{(1) Detecting completeness.} In the subsolver version,
``\textsf{unsat}'' announces that all cells are covered. Here one would
like to wait until the SAT solver proves $\neg h_k$ outright --- but a SAT
solver only derives what the search forces it to derive, and nothing forces
it to keep trying $h_k = \true$; runs livelocked. The repair is the
\emph{driver lemma}
$\ell \Rightarrow (p[\vv] \vee \mathit{star}_k \vee h_k)$:
if the input asserts $\ell$ and the model certifies it by neither
$p[\vv]$ nor $\mathit{star}_k$, the model \emph{must} set $h_k = \true$,
i.e.\ exhibit a fresh cell. When all cells are covered, the $h_k$ branch
becomes contradictory and only the certified branches survive.

\emph{(2) The empty sum.} The first-round driver must not be
$\ell \Rightarrow (p[\vv] \vee h_0)$. Counterexample: take the predicate
$p(x) = x \ge 1 \wedge x \le 0$, which no point satisfies, so
$\Sstar = \{\vzero\}$; and an input asserting $\ell$ plus $v_1 = 0$.
Then $\ell$ is \emph{true} ($\vv = \vzero$ is the empty sum), but
$p[\vv]$ is false and $h_0$ is contradictory ($\mathit{base}$ is
unsatisfiable) --- the driver would force $\neg\ell$: a wrong
\textsf{unsat}. The star branch must therefore always include the empty
sum, $\vv = \vzero$. This bug was found by an adversarial review and is
now the regression test \texttt{empty\_pred\_sat.smt2}.

\emph{(3) Solver pops.} In incremental usage (\texttt{push}/\texttt{pop}
by the \emph{user}), lemmas are retracted on pop but the extension's
bookkeeping survives, so the enumeration constraints silently vanished
(observed as a wrong \textsf{sat}). Fix: re-submit all enumeration lemmas
every round; the solver's lemma cache drops duplicates for free and
re-sends them after a pop.}

\emph{Measured result:} sound after the fixes, but clearly worse (447 vs.\
463): every refinement round now drags the entire input problem along, and
only one cell is harvested per full-effort check. The lasting value is the
\emph{decoupling lesson} --- enumeration and the main search starve each
other when coupled --- which returns with the sign flipped in
Section~\ref{sec:sat}.

\subsection{Generalizing the discovered cells (now the default)}

Step 2 of the lazy loop fixes \emph{every} atom of $\mathit{base}$ to its
model value. That makes cells as small as possible --- bad, because more
cells means more rounds.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} Recall from the round-1 walk-through that the model
$\vy = (4,0)$ produced
\[
D_1 \;=\; x \ge 1 \;\wedge\; y = 0 \;\wedge\; \neg(x = 0) \;\wedge\; y < 2 .
\]
The conjuncts $\neg(x = 0)$ and $y < 2$ come from atoms of the
\emph{other} cell and are redundant here. Now imagine the predicate also
contained an unrelated \textsf{ite} over a variable $w$: its atoms (say
$w \ge 5$ and its negation) would also be fixed, splitting the one
horizontal cell into \emph{two} cells ($w \ge 5$ and $w < 5$) that the
loop must discover separately. With $m$ irrelevant atoms, one logical cell
shatters into up to $2^m$ discovered cells.
\end{exbox}

\emph{Boolean generalization} repairs this with a \emph{prime implicant}
computation. (An implicant of a formula is a conjunction of literals that
forces the formula to be true; it is \emph{prime} if dropping any literal
breaks that. Example: for $a \wedge (b \vee c)$, the conjunction
$a \wedge b \wedge c$ is an implicant; $a \wedge b$ is a prime implicant.)
The algorithm walks over the kept literals and, for each, temporarily
marks its atom \emph{unknown} and re-evaluates $\mathit{base}$ in
three-valued logic --- where $\wedge$ yields \false{} if any conjunct is
\false{}, \emph{unknown} if any is unknown (and none \false), and \true{}
otherwise; $\vee$ is the mirror image. If the formula still evaluates to
\true, every possible value of that atom keeps $\mathit{base}$ true, so
the literal is dropped for good.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} On
$D_1$ above, with $\mathit{base} = (x{\ge}1 \wedge y{=}0) \vee (x{=}0 \wedge y{\ge}2)$:
marking $\neg(x{=}0)$'s atom unknown leaves the first disjunct
$\true \wedge \true = \true$, so the disjunction is \true{} --- drop it.
Marking $y < 2$'s atom ($y \ge 2$) unknown: same --- drop it. Marking
$x \ge 1$ unknown: the first disjunct becomes \emph{unknown}, the second is
\false, so the disjunction is \emph{unknown} --- keep it. Result:
$D_1' = x \ge 1 \wedge y = 0$, exactly the logical cell.
\end{exbox}

Soundness: three-valued \true{} under a partial assignment means
\emph{every} completion of the unknowns satisfies $\mathit{base}$ ---
i.e.\ the kept conjunction still implies $\mathit{base}$, so the cell's
points all lie in $\Sset$ and the star constraints built from it never
overshoot. The model point stays inside the (larger) cell, so asserting
its negation still makes progress. And if two generalized cells overlap,
nothing breaks: the correctness of the cell-by-cell star encoding only
needs the cells to \emph{cover} $\Sset$, not to be disjoint --- a sum
grouped by cells stays a sum.

\emph{Measured result:} the best general-purpose improvement of the whole
project --- discovered cells halve in size, 466/480 solved, $4.4\times$
faster on the commonly solved instances. Enabled by default.

\emph{Semantic generalization} (optional, off) pushes further: drop a
literal whenever the rest \emph{arithmetically} implies $\mathit{base}$,
which the propositional view cannot see.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} $\mathit{base} = x \ge 1 \wedge x + y \ge 1 \wedge y \ge 0$,
model $(1,0)$. All three atoms are top-level conjuncts, so the
propositional check keeps all three. But
$x \ge 1 \wedge y \ge 0$ already implies $x + y \ge 1$ \emph{as
arithmetic}. The implication is verified with a probe subsolver holding
$\neg\mathit{base}$: check, under assumptions $\{x \ge 1,\ y \ge 0\}$
--- \textsf{unsat} confirms the implication, so $x + y \ge 1$ is dropped
and the cell fattens.
\end{exbox}

\emph{Measured result:} negative on this suite (465 solved, $2.25\times$
slower) --- each probe costs a solver call, and fatter cells have larger
Hilbert bases, hence more multipliers. Kept as an option; its
\emph{probe-validation} pattern (``a claim about all of $\mathit{base}$
$=$ one \textsf{unsat} answer'') is reused by the cut synthesis in
Section~\ref{sec:cuts}.

\subsection{Constant-size lemmas via partial sums}

Round $k$'s guarded lemma contains the sum constraints over \emph{all} $k$
cells, so the total text emitted over $K$ rounds grows like $K^2$. One can
instead define \emph{running totals} once per round ---
$P_k = P_{k-1} + (\text{round } k\text{'s contribution})$, over fresh
unknowns $P_k$ --- and shrink the guarded lemma to the constant-size
$\vv = P_k$. The definitions are safe to assert unconditionally: they only
constrain fresh symbols (set all multipliers to $0$ and every $P_k$ to
$\vzero$ to satisfy them in any model).

\emph{Measured result:} clearly negative --- memory rose (4.4 vs.\
3.3\,GB on the hard instance) and 13 fewer instances were solved. Two
measured reasons. First, the per-cell machinery (multiplier bounds,
coupling implications, definition rows) is itself tens of lemmas per
round; the quadratic term we removed was not the dominant cost. Second, a
subtle search effect: when the solver explores
$\ell = \false$ under the guarded equivalence, it must falsify the star
formula; falsifying the old ``fat'' star was cheap (violate any one
$\mu \ge 0$), but falsifying $\vv = P_k$ means asserting a
\emph{disequality} $v_i \ne P_k[i]$, which integer reasoning handles by
case-splitting ($<$ or $>$) --- the count of those splits exploded
($14 \to 663$). \textbf{Lesson: lemma \emph{shape} affects search, not
just lemma size.} This negative result redirected attention from memory to
the two real bottlenecks, treated next.

\section{Hard satisfiable instances: aim the search, then finish the job}
\label{sec:sat}

The hardest \textsf{sat} instances (the \texttt{fol\_0000099--106}
family) survived everything above; the flagship \texttt{fol\_0000100}
timed out at 600\,s in every configuration, running ${\sim}1000$
refinement rounds. Three measured facts explain it, and each dictates one
component of the cure.

\textbf{Fact 1: a one-summand witness does not exist.} The input's side
constraints force $\vv$ to be a sum of at least two members (checked
standalone: side constraints $\wedge\ p[\vv]$ is \textsf{unsat}), so the
model-value shortcut cannot fire early; the star is unavoidable.

\textbf{Fact 2: undirected discovery wanders.} The subsolver's model is
\emph{any} point not yet covered --- nothing relates it to $\vv$. Traced
on \texttt{fol\_0000100}: the cells needed to decompose $\vv$ appear
within ${\sim}30$ rounds when the search is aimed (below), while the
unaimed search ran $580$ cones without assembling them (proved by an
\textsf{unsat}-core analysis in the next section).

\textbf{Fact 3: the endgame is starved.} Even with the right cells
present, certifying \textsf{sat} means the \emph{main} solver must find
integer values for hundreds of multipliers --- an integer program. Integer
reasoning in \cvc finishes with \emph{branch-and-bound}: if the relaxed
(fractional) solution has $\mu = 1.5$, the solver splits on
$\mu \le 1 \vee \mu \ge 2$ and recurses. Two measured interferences kept
this from ever completing: (i)~the star extension runs \emph{before}
branch-and-bound each round, sees a fractional candidate (which fails the
model-value shortcut), and immediately harvests another cell --- rewriting
and growing the integer program before the splitting could finish;
(ii)~the star's many fresh equality atoms enter the SAT solver with
default (false-leaning) phases, so some atom is assigned \false{} early,
which \emph{unit-propagates} the guard $g_k$ to \false{} long before any
decision on $g_k$ --- measured: across 643 rounds the guard was never
\true{} even once, so the solver never even attempted the star. A phase
\emph{preference} on $g_k$ is powerless against propagation.

The cure has three cooperating parts.

\subsection{Part 1: guided enumeration (default on)}

\begin{proposition}
If $\vv = \vec{s}_1 + \cdots + \vec{s}_k$ with all $\vec{s}_j \ge 0$,
then every summand satisfies $\vec{s}_j \le \vv$ coordinate-wise.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Removing non-negative summands never increases any coordinate.
\end{proof}

So only cells intersecting the box $\vy \le \vv$ can contribute to a
decomposition of $\vv$. The subsolver query is therefore first asked under
the assumptions $\{\,y_i \le \hat v_i\,\}$, where $\hat{\vv}$ is the value
of $\vv$ in the current candidate model; if the assumed query is
\textsf{unsat}, it is retried without assumptions. The fallback keeps the
completeness signal intact: ``\textsf{unsat} without assumptions'' still
means full coverage.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} Running example,
$p(x,y) = (x \ge 1 \wedge y = 0) \vee (x = 0 \wedge y \ge 2)$, with the
input forcing $\vv = (5,0)$. The assumptions are
$y_1 \le 5 \wedge y_2 \le 0$. The vertical cell needs $y_2 \ge 2$ ---
excluded outright. The enumeration will only ever look at the horizontal
cell: the single cell that can decompose $(5,0)$. On
\texttt{fol\_0000100}, 7 of the 14 indicator coordinates of $\vv$ are
$0$, so the assumptions pin those $y_i$ to $0$ and the search space
collapses onto the relevant region.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{Part 2: a decoupled endgame (default on, every 10 cells)}

Every $N$ newly discovered cells, the extension builds a \emph{fresh}
subsolver and asks one clean question:
\[
F_0 \;\wedge\; \mathit{star}_k \;\;\stackrel{?}{=}\;\; \textsf{sat},
\]
where $F_0$ is the set of arithmetic facts \emph{fixed at decision
level~0} in the main solver (recall the trail example: level-0 facts hold
in every model --- they are exactly the input-entailed constraints). A
fresh solver has no history --- no retired guards, no unlucky phases, no
half-grown integer program --- and runs branch-and-bound to completion in
milliseconds. If the answer is \textsf{sat}, its model is a complete
witness: concrete values for $\vv$ and for every multiplier.

\why{Both design choices were forced by failures.

\emph{Why only level-0 facts?} The first version asserted \emph{all}
facts currently on the trail. On \texttt{fol\_0000100} the trail happened
to contain the branch decision $n = 1$ (part of an abandoned search
region that was already known to be contradictory), and every endgame
query inherited it --- so every query answered \textsf{unsat} even though
the discovered cells could perfectly well decompose a valid $\vv$ with,
say, $n = 4$. Level-0 facts are exactly those every model must satisfy:
the query becomes ``can the current cells justify \emph{some} valid
$\vv$,'' which is the right question.

\emph{Why a fresh solver?} The main solver could in principle answer the
same question --- Fact 3 shows why it never does: the question keeps being
rewritten under it. This is the decoupling lesson from the failed
main-solver experiment, applied in the profitable direction.}

\subsection{Part 3: feeding the witness back as decisions}

The witness must now reach the \emph{main} solver. Encoding it as a lemma
$d \Rightarrow (x_1 = c_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_m = c_m)$, with $d$ a
fresh Boolean whose phase is preferred \true, measurably never works.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (why the hint lemma never fires).} Hint:
$d \Rightarrow (a = 2 \wedge b = 3)$, i.e.\ clauses
$(\neg d \vee a{=}2)$ and $(\neg d \vee b{=}3)$. Suppose the trail
already contains $a = 1$ --- some earlier decision or propagation made for
unrelated reasons. Then the atom $a{=}2$ is \false{} and the first clause
\emph{unit-propagates} $\neg d$. The phase preference on $d$ is
irrelevant: $d$ is never \emph{decided}, it is forced. With $m \approx
230$ pinned values, \emph{some} variable is always already bound
differently, so this happens every single round --- measured: the hint
guard was \false{} at every check, forever.
\end{exbox}

The fix uses a different solver facility: a \emph{decision strategy}. SAT
solvers let theory modules dictate the next decision variable(s), taking
priority over the built-in heuristic. While a hint is active, our strategy
feeds the solver $d$ first and then each equality $x_i = c_i$, as
\emph{decisions}. A decision happens at the top of the trail --- nothing is
``already bound differently'' there; the solver either completes the
witness assignment (and the model-value shortcut then certifies
\textsf{sat}, since the witness satisfies $\mathit{star}_k$), or hits a
genuine conflict whose reason is recorded and search continues. Only one
hint is active per literal --- a newer witness retires the previous guard
with $\neg d_{\mathit{old}}$ --- because two witnesses pin the same
variables to different values and would fight.

The hint lemma itself is sound for the usual reason: $d$ is fresh, so any
model extends by $d = \false$.

\emph{Measured result:} \texttt{fol\_0000100} went from a 600-second
timeout (in every prior configuration) to \textsf{sat} in $0.7$ seconds,
model-checked; the whole hard \textsf{sat} family followed; 476/480
overall, with four \textsf{unsat} instances left.

\section{Hard unsatisfiable instances: over-approximation by cuts}
\label{sec:cuts}

For an \textsf{unsat} instance the loop so far has only one exit: complete
the enumeration and emit the exact equivalence. On the four remaining
instances that is hopeless --- the cell count is astronomic (the eager
mode times out too). What is missing is the \emph{other} half of the
approximation recipe: a way to \emph{over}-approximate $\Sstar$, so that
``input $\wedge$ over-approximation \textsf{unsat}'' refutes $\ell$
\emph{without} enumerating anything.

\subsection{Cuts: inequalities that survive addition}

\begin{proposition}
Let $\vc$ be an integer vector. If $\vc \cdot \vy \ge 0$ holds for every
$\vy \in \Sset$ (a \emph{homogeneous} valid inequality --- no constant
term), then $\vc \cdot \vv \ge 0$ holds for every $\vv \in \Sstar$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $\vv = \sum_j \vec{s}_j$ then
$\vc \cdot \vv = \sum_j \vc \cdot \vec{s}_j \ge 0$, a sum of non-negative
numbers; and the empty sum gives $\vc \cdot \vzero = 0 \ge 0$.
\end{proof}

We call such an inequality a \emph{cut}. A cut is a sound,
\emph{unconditional} lemma about $\vv$: no guard, no enumeration, no
completeness requirement.

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} Let $\Sset' = \{(1,1)\}$, so
$(S')^{*} = \{(k,k) \mid k \ge 0\}$ --- the diagonal. The inequality
$x - y \ge 0$ is valid on $\Sset'$ (it holds at $(1,1)$) and homogeneous,
so it lifts: every $(k,k)$ satisfies $k - k \ge 0$. Now suppose the input
asserts $v_1 = 3 \wedge v_2 = 5$ and $\ell$. The single cut lemma
$v_1 - v_2 \ge 0$ contradicts $3 - 5 = -2 \ge 0$ --- the solver answers
\textsf{unsat} immediately, having never built a single cell cone.

\textbf{Counter-example (why homogeneity is required).} The inequality
$x \le 1$ \emph{also} holds for every member of $\Sset' = \{(1,1)\}$, but
it must \emph{not} be lifted: $(2,2) \in (S')^{*}$ violates it. The
difference: $x \le 1$ has a constant term ($x - 1 \le 0$), and constants
do not scale when summands are added. The same trap in the running
example $p(x,y) = (x{\ge}1 \wedge y{=}0) \vee (x{=}0 \wedge y{\ge}2)$:
every point of $\Sset$ satisfies $x + y \ge 1$, yet the empty sum
$\vzero \in \Sstar$ does not. Homogeneous ($\vc \cdot \vy \ge 0$) is
exactly the shape that survives addition.
\end{exbox}

Why should such cuts refute our benchmarks? Because their \textsf{unsat}
arguments are \emph{counting} arguments: ``every summand lacks at least
one of these five features, but the input demands totals only achievable
if almost no summand lacks any.'' Summed per-summand inequalities are
precisely homogeneous cuts.

\subsection{Conditional cuts: exploiting forced zeros}

\begin{proposition}
Suppose the input forces $v_i = 0$. Then every summand of every
decomposition of $\vv$ has $y_i = 0$ (coordinates are non-negative and
sums cannot cancel). Hence an inequality valid merely on the
\emph{restriction} $\Sset \cap \{\vy \mid y_i = 0\}$ is still a sound cut
for this $\vv$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example.} In the running example, suppose the input forces
$v_2 = 0$. Every summand then has $y = 0$, i.e.\ comes from the
horizontal cell. On that restriction the inequality $x - y \ge 0$ is
valid (it fails on the vertical cell, so it is \emph{not} valid on all of
$\Sset$) --- and it may be soundly asserted for $\vv$.

On the real benchmark \texttt{fol\_0000107} (multiset encoding) the
refuting cut is
$4 u_{10} - u_{14} - u_{17} - u_{20} - u_{23} - u_{26} \ge 0$
(``the five tracked totals never exceed four universes''), which is valid
\emph{only} where six comparison-indicator coordinates are $0$ --- and
the input pins exactly those coordinates of $\vv$ to the constant $0$.
Without conditional cuts this instance is out of reach; with them it
refutes in $2$ seconds.
\end{exbox}

Forced zeros are easy to detect: input equalities like $u_{13} = 0$ are
substituted into the literal by preprocessing, so the corresponding
argument of $\ell$ is literally the constant $0$.

\subsection{Finding cuts automatically}

A cut useful for refutation must do two jobs: be \emph{valid} on (the
restriction of) $\Sset$, and \emph{reject} the values of $\vv$ the input
allows. The synthesis loop interleaves both, in the style known as
counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS): propose a candidate
from finitely many constraints, check it against the real object, and turn
each failure into a new constraint.

Concretely, whenever the endgame query
$F_0 \wedge \mathit{star}_k$ answers \textsf{unsat} (``the discovered
cells cannot justify $\ell$ --- maybe nothing can''), the extension runs:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Target.} Find a model of $F_0 \wedge C$, where $C$ are the
  cuts found so far. If there is none, the already-emitted cut lemmas
  contradict the input by themselves; the main solver will answer
  \textsf{unsat} on its own, and synthesis stops. Otherwise read the
  target $\hat{\vv}$.
\item \textbf{Candidate.} Solve a small integer query for coefficients
  $\vc$ such that: $\sum_i |c_i| \le K$ for an escalating budget
  $K \in \{4,9,16,30\}$; $\vc \cdot \vec{q} \ge 0$ for every known sample
  point $\vec{q}$ of the restricted predicate; and
  $\vc \cdot \hat{\vv} \le -1$ (the candidate must reject the target).
\item \textbf{Validate.} Ask the probe (the $\mathit{base}$ oracle reused
  from semantic generalization): is
  $\mathit{base} \wedge \bigwedge_{v_i \equiv 0} y_i{=}0 \wedge
  \vc \cdot \vy \le -1$ satisfiable? If \textsf{unsat}, the candidate is
  a valid cut --- emit $\vc \cdot \vv \ge 0$ and return to step 1. If
  \textsf{sat}, the probe's model is a genuine point of the restricted
  predicate violating the candidate --- add it to the samples and return
  to step 2.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{exbox}
\textbf{Example (one full pass).} $\Sset' = \{(1,1)\}$, input
$v_1 = 3 \wedge v_2 = 5$, no samples yet.
\emph{Target:} $\hat{\vv} = (3,5)$.
\emph{Candidate:} with no sample constraints, the solver may propose
$\vc = (0,-1)$, i.e.\ ``$-y \ge 0$'', which indeed rejects the target
($-5 \le -1$).
\emph{Validate:} is some point of $\Sset'$ with $-y \le -1$? Yes,
$(1,1)$ --- the candidate is not valid; $(1,1)$ becomes a sample.
\emph{Candidate again:} now require $c_1 + c_2 \ge 0$ (from the sample)
and $3 c_1 + 5 c_2 \le -1$ (target); the budget-minimal solution is
$\vc = (1,-1)$.
\emph{Validate:} no point of $\Sset'$ has $x - y \le -1$ --- valid. Emit
$v_1 - v_2 \ge 0$.
\emph{Target again:} $F_0 \wedge (v_1 - v_2 \ge 0)$ with
$v_1{=}3, v_2{=}5$ is \textsf{unsat} --- done; the input is refuted.
\end{exbox}

\why{Each ingredient of the loop closes a failure observed without it.

\emph{The sparsity budget.} With 21 coordinates and coefficients in
$[-10,10]$, there are ${\sim}21^{21}$ candidate vectors; an unbounded
search returns scattered dense candidates, the oracle refutes them one
counterexample at a time, and 40 iterations made no visible progress. The
cuts that exist in practice are \emph{sparse} (few nonzero, small
coefficients --- like every example above), and bounding
$\sum_i |c_i|$ makes the candidate space tiny. An offline replica of the
exact loop converged on \texttt{fol\_0000107} in 8 cuts and ${\sim}33$
candidate--validate iterations once the budget was added.

\emph{Level-0 facts for the target}, for the same reason as the endgame:
a branch-pinned target sends the synthesis chasing values no model needs.

\emph{Sample points yes, directions no.} Sample points are seeded from
the module generators of already-discovered cells: a generator is a real
point of $\Sset$, and if its restricted coordinates are zero it lies in
the restricted region, so requiring $\vc \cdot \vec{q} \ge 0$ on it is
exactly right and saves validate-rounds. The cells' \emph{ray} directions
must NOT be used the same way, and this was a real bug: consider a cell
whose pinned indicator is $u_{13} = 1$. Every \emph{ray} of that cell has
$0$ in the $u_{13}$ coordinate --- moving along a direction never changes
a pinned equality --- so the ray slips past the ``restricted coordinates
are zero'' filter while describing motion \emph{inside a cell that is
entirely outside the restriction}. Requiring $\vc \cdot \vec{r} \ge 0$ on
such rays excluded precisely the valid conditional cut on
\texttt{fol\_0000107}: the in-solver loop failed while the ray-free
offline replica succeeded, and removing the ray constraints reconciled
the two. Unbounded directions are still handled soundly: if a candidate
is violated far out along some direction of the restricted region, the
validation probe simply returns a far-out \emph{point} as the
counterexample.}

\emph{Measured result:} the four remaining instances refute in
$0.2$--$2.0$ seconds; nothing else on the suite is affected.

discrepancy was observed anywhere: neither between any two of our

\section{Takeaways}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Measure, then optimize.} The two memory-motivated designs
  (push/pop, partial sums) attacked a bottleneck that profiling refuted;
  the real walls were the \emph{order} of cell discovery (sat side) and
  the missing over-approximation (unsat side).
\item \textbf{Approximate from both sides.} Under-approximations certify
  \textsf{sat} as soon as enough cells exist; homogeneous (conditionally
  valid) cuts certify \textsf{unsat} without ever completing the
  enumeration. The lazy star needs both.
\item \textbf{Decouple searches that starve each other.} In-search
  refinement kept rewriting the main solver's integer program; a fresh
  subsolver answers the same question in milliseconds. And when feeding a
  many-variable witness back, use a decision strategy --- propagation
  defeats phase preferences every time.
\item \textbf{Negative results pay rent.} The probe oracle built for the
  losing semantic generalization became the validity engine of the winning
  cut synthesis; the failed main-solver experiment supplied the
  decoupling principle behind the endgame.
\end{itemize}
